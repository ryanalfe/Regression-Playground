HOW TO READ IT

A good fit is only the beginning.

The line minimizes the sum of squared vertical distances from the points.

R² describes the share of variation in y explained by this fitted line. It does not establish cause and effect or guarantee accurate future predictions.

Residuals show what the line misses. Look for patterns, unusually large errors, and influential observations.

Start with the equation, then check what the line leaves behind. Open a lesson for an explanation and a small experiment.

01 · The equation: what are we predicting?

ŷ = b₀ + b₁x

x is the input. y is the observed outcome. ŷ (pronounced “y-hat”) is the outcome predicted by the line. The intercept, b₀, sets the line’s height at x = 0. The slope, b₁, describes the predicted change in y when x increases by one unit.

In the starting advertising example, the fitted equation is approximately ŷ = 11.79 + 0.77x. Both axes are in thousands of dollars: another $1,000 of advertising is associated with roughly $770 higher predicted sales. That is a description of this illustrative dataset, not proof that spending more causes sales to rise.

Try it: Move a point and watch the equation change. A negative slope means predicted y decreases as x increases.

02 · Least squares: why this particular line?

For each point, calculate the vertical distance between its observed y and the line’s predicted ŷ. Square those distances and add them up. Ordinary least squares chooses the intercept and slope that make that total as small as possible.

Squaring prevents positive and negative errors from canceling and gives large errors more weight. “Best” here means best under this mathematical rule; it does not mean the model is the right explanation for the real world.

Try it: Turn on “Show residuals.” Move one point far from the line and notice how the fit responds.

03 · R²: how much variation does the line explain?

R² = 1 − SSE / SST

SSE is the sum of squared residuals from the fitted line. SST is the sum of squared distances from the mean of y. R² compares the fitted line with simply predicting the mean for every observation.

An R² of 0.92 means the line accounts for about 92% of the observed variation in y in this dataset. It does not mean predictions are 92% accurate, that 92% of points lie on the line, or that x causes y.

For the ordinary least-squares model with an intercept used here, in-sample R² lies between 0 and 1 when y varies. If all y values are identical, SST is zero and R² is undefined. Predictions on new data can perform much worse than the training fit.

Try it: Load “A hidden curve.” A low R² for a straight line can coexist with a strong nonlinear relationship.

04 · Residuals: what is the model missing?

Residual = y − ŷ

A positive residual means the observation lies above the line: the model underpredicts it. A negative residual means the model overpredicts it.

Scattered residuals around zero with a roughly similar spread are consistent with a useful linear model, but do not prove that its assumptions hold. A curved pattern suggests missing nonlinearity. A widening spread suggests nonconstant error variance. Time-related dependence requires a separate check against observation order or time.

Try it: Compare the residual plots for “Advertising & sales” and “A hidden curve.” Describe the pattern before looking at R².

05 · Outliers, leverage, and influence

An observation with an unusually large residual is unusual in y relative to the fitted model. A high-leverage point has an x value far from the other x values. An influential point substantially changes the fitted model when it is removed or moved.

These concepts overlap, but are different. A distant x value provides leverage; whether the point pulls the line strongly also depends on its y value. An unusual point may be a data error, an important subgroup, or a valid rare event. Investigate it before deciding to exclude it.

Try it: Load “One influential point.” Move the isolated rightmost point, then delete it using the keyboard. Compare the slope before and after.

06 · Association, causation, and extrapolation

A regression fit alone does not establish cause and effect. For example, a business might advertise more during busy seasons. Seasonality could influence both advertising and sales. A causal claim needs a credible research design and assumptions, such as an appropriately randomized experiment.

Interpolation predicts within the observed x range. Extrapolation predicts beyond it, where the observed pattern may not continue. The line shown across the entire grid is a mathematical extension, not evidence that all those predictions are reliable.

Try it: Build a dataset with points clustered on the left. Notice how much of the displayed line has no observations nearby to support it.

07 · Fit is not certainty

Two points with different x values determine a line exactly. If their y values differ, R² equals 1, even though two observations provide very little evidence about a broader population.

A fitted line can be computed without normally distributed errors. Standard uncertainty calculations introduce further conditions: a correctly specified mean relationship, independent errors, and—for conventional standard errors—constant error variance. Normal errors support exact small-sample t and F inference. Real analyses may require different methods when these conditions fail.

This playground shows a fitted line and descriptive diagnostics. It does not calculate confidence intervals, prediction intervals, p-values, or performance on held-out data. A confidence interval for a mean response and a prediction interval for an individual outcome answer different questions; the latter also includes individual outcome variation.

Try it: Add two points in “Your own points,” then add several more that depart from the line. Watch a perfect initial fit lose its apparent certainty.

Explain a model in four sentences

  1. Question: What outcome am I trying to understand, and why?
  2. Relationship: What does the slope mean in the original units?
  3. Diagnostics: What do R² and the residuals reveal?
  4. Limits: What cannot be concluded, and what evidence would I collect next?
